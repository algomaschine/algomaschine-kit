    The AlgoMaschine method

    How every result on this dashboard is produced and audited: the principles, the validation toolkit, and the tests that decide whether a backtest deserves belief, with their formulas and the reasons behind them.

    AlgoMaschine ResearchThe generic part of the AlgoMaschine white paper

    1Why most backtests are wrong

    A backtest replays a trading rule on history. It is the only laboratory a trader has, and a treacherous one. Markets offer a small signal buried in a great deal of noise; history is a single path that cannot be run again; and the researcher can try as many variations as time allows. Each of these on its own inflates results. Together they make most backtests, including many published ones, wrong (Harvey, Liu and Zhu, 2016; Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu, 2014).

    In plain words

    If a thousand people each toss a coin ten times, a few will throw nine or ten heads. That does not make them skilled tossers. A backtest that is the best of many attempts is one of those lucky tossers, unless we can show otherwise.

    For the quant

    With \(T\) years of data, the standard error of an annualised Sharpe ratio is roughly \(1/\sqrt{T}\). The largest of \(N\) independent estimates whose true value is zero is then, to first order,

    $$E\Big[\max_{n\le N}\widehat{SR}_n\Big]\;\approx\;\sqrt{\frac{2\ln N}{T}}.$$

    Trying \(N=45\) variations on \(T=5\) years yields a best Sharpe ratio of about 1.2 from pure noise. Read the other way, the formula gives the minimum backtest length needed for a given number of trials (Section 3).

    Four explanations other than skill

    Every good-looking backtest must be defended against four rival explanations, plus one question of fragility. They organise the whole of this paper and of the audit in Section 3.

    1. Luck. The record is too short or too noisy to tell a real edge from chance.
    2. The search. The result is the best of many tries, and the best of many tries always looks good.
    3. The market. The profit comes from being long in a rising market, not from timing.
    4. Errors. The backtest used information that was not available at the time, skipped costs, or trusted bad data.
    5. Fragility. The result depends on a handful of days, one year, leverage, or costs that are too kind.

    Our rules of engagement

    1. Write it down first. The hypothesis, the parameter grid, the costs and the tests are fixed in a specification before any result is computed (SPEC.md, frozen with make spec-lock). Changes after that are disclosed and counted.
    2. Count every trial. A ledger records every configuration ever tried. The deflated Sharpe ratio (Section 2) charges the final result for all of them.
    3. Walk forward. Every choice for a given year is made with data that ends before that year starts.
    4. Test the testing machine. Before any real-data run, the pipeline must find nothing in pure noise and must find an effect we plant on purpose (Section 2.7).
    5. Audit anything that looks good. The forensic audit (Section 3) is run on every attractive result.
    6. Paper before money. A backtest, however clean, is evidence about the past. Only new data can confirm an edge.
    From our own mistakes
    • A clock error created an edge. An early version of one of our systems, a large rule search, found that currency moves seemed to lead the US stock market. The lead came from the data: before 2021 the vendor's five-minute FX bars carried New York wall-clock times labelled as UTC. The data loader now re-stamps those clocks, and a quality gate checks, year by year, that the US payrolls release shows up at 08:30 New York time in the data.
    • The signal price was the entry price. A month-end reversal after the WM/R fixing looked like a new edge (t = 4.5) until we noticed that the signal and the entry used the same fixing price. With a five-minute entry delay, only one currency pair survived, at t = 3.3. Since then a signal is never allowed to trade at the price that created it.
    • Data artefacts in emerging markets. A later search's headline out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.95 came mostly from emerging-market currencies at their local open, where quotes are likely indicative rather than tradable, and from currency momentum computed without the interest carry. Without them the Sharpe ratio fell to about 0.1.

    Each of these is now a standing check: clock tests in the data gate, the execution-delay and point-in-time tests of the audit, and data flags on every alpha.

    2The validation toolkit

    This section describes the methods that protect every choice made inside the models. Section 3 describes the audit that judges the result. Most of these methods come from López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning (2018, "AFML") and Machine Learning for Asset Managers (2020).

    2.1 Purged cross-validation with an embargo

    Motivation

    Ordinary cross-validation assumes the observations are independent. Trades are not: a 21-day trade entered on 3 March and one entered on 5 March share almost all their outcome. If one sits in the training set and the other in the test set, the model is tested on an answer it has already seen. Purging removes such overlaps; an embargo after each test block also removes trades that could have learnt from the test period through serial correlation.

    Let trade \(i\) span \([e_i,t_{1,i}]\) and a test block span \([a,b]\). A training trade is kept only if

    $$[e_i,\,t_{1,i}]\cap[a,\,b]=\varnothing\quad\text{and}\quad e_i\notin(b,\,b+h],\qquad h=0.01\,T.$$

    2.2 Sample uniqueness

    Overlapping trades also carry less information than their number suggests. With \(c_t\) the number of trades active on day \(t\), the average uniqueness of trade \(i\) is

    $$\bar u_i=\frac{1}{t_{1,i}-e_i+1}\sum_{t=e_i}^{t_{1,i}}\frac{1}{c_t}.$$

    It weights each trade when fitting the meta-model, and the mean uniqueness sets the size of each tree's bootstrap sample, so that the forest does not treat twenty overlapping copies of one market episode as twenty independent facts (AFML ch. 4).

    2.3 Combinatorial purged cross-validation

    Motivation

    A walk-forward test produces a single path through history, and one path can be lucky. Combinatorial purged cross-validation (AFML ch. 12) splits the history into \(N\) groups, uses every choice of \(k\) of them as the test set, and stitches the test results into \(\varphi=\frac{k}{N}\binom{N}{k}\) complete backtest paths. With \(N=10\) and \(k=2\) that is 45 splits and 9 paths. A robust rule shows most of its paths in profit.

    2.4 The probability of backtest overfitting

    Motivation

    A calibration that picks the best of many configurations can be picking noise. The probability of backtest overfitting (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu, 2017) measures how often the configuration that looks best on one half of history turns out below average on the other half, using every possible half.

    Algorithm 4 · combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) M = T × N matrix of daily returns, one column per configuration split the rows into S = 16 blocks of equal length for each of the C(16, 8) = 12,870 ways to pick 8 blocks as "in sample": n* = configuration with the best in-sample Sharpe ratio ω = rank of n* among all N configurations out of sample, divided by N + 1 λ = ln( ω / (1 − ω) ) # below 0: worse than the median out of sample PBO = share of the 12,870 splits with λ ≤ 0

    A PBO near 0 means the selection carries real information; 0.5 means it is no better than a coin; above 0.5, the best in sample tends to be worse than average out of sample. PBO says nothing about whether the chosen rule makes money, only whether choosing among the configurations was meaningful.

    2.5 The probabilistic and deflated Sharpe ratios

    For per-period Sharpe ratio \(\widehat{SR}\) over \(T\) observations with skewness \(\hat\gamma_3\) and kurtosis \(\hat\gamma_4\), the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds a benchmark \(SR^\ast\) is (Bailey and López de Prado, 2012)

    $$\widehat{PSR}(SR^\ast)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{(\widehat{SR}-SR^\ast)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1-\hat\gamma_3\,\widehat{SR}+\frac{\hat\gamma_4-1}{4}\,\widehat{SR}^2}}\right).$$

    Fat tails and negative skew widen the uncertainty, so the same Sharpe ratio is less convincing for a strategy with crash risk. The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey and López de Prado, 2014) sets the benchmark to the best Sharpe ratio expected from \(N\) trials with no skill, where \(V\) is the variance of the Sharpe ratios across the trials and \(\gamma\approx0.5772\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant:

    $$SR^\ast=\sqrt{V}\left((1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big[1-\frac1N\Big]+\gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big[1-\frac{1}{N e}\Big]\right),\qquad DSR=\widehat{PSR}(SR^\ast).$$

    We require \(DSR\ge 0.95\). The number of trials \(N\) comes from the ledger: every configuration of every model plus every earlier selection process of the factory (the count is shown next to every deflated Sharpe ratio).

    In plain words

    The deflated Sharpe ratio asks: "given how many things you tried, what is the chance that this result is better than the best of that many coin-tossers?" Below 95%, we do not call it evidence.

    2.6 Feature importance

    Mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) measures how much the meta-model's out-of-fold log-loss worsens when one input is randomly shuffled, under purged cross-validation (AFML ch. 8). An input the model truly uses shows a clear increase; values near zero mean it adds nothing. In the S&P Lab every input of the wave model's meta-model was near zero: the second opinion learnt almost nothing, which is itself a finding.

    2.7 Testing the testing machine: the pre-run review

    Motivation

    A validation pipeline can have bugs too, and a bug in the pipeline invalidates every result it produces. So before any run on real data, the pipeline is run on data where the right answer is known.

    • Look-ahead probes. Future data are perturbed and every calibration decision before the perturbation must stay the same; turning points must be used only after confirmation; the fractional difference must be causal; purging must remove every overlapping label.
    • Pure noise. The market's returns with random signs contain no pattern. The pipeline must find none, and its out-of-sample Sharpe ratio must be near zero. In the S&P Lab it was 0.01, while the configurations it chose had a median cross-validated Sharpe ratio of 0.40: a live demonstration of selection bias, which walk-forward testing then removed.
    • A planted effect. A pattern with a known edge (t ≈ 5 over the full sample) is inserted. The calibration must find it.

    All checks must pass before make walkforward is allowed to run on real data (make review). A planted effect must be large enough to be detectable at all; a test that is resized after its first run is disclosed in the specification.

    3The forensic audit

    Motivation

    The methods of Section 2 protect the choices made inside a model. They cannot tell whether the final result deserves belief. A result can survive all of them and still be explained by luck, by the size of the search that produced it, by the market's own rise, by an error, or by a few fortunate days. The audit asks those questions directly, with tests taken from the literature on backtest overfitting and data snooping. The pass rules were fixed in code before the S&P results were audited, so they cannot be bent to fit.

    3.2 The key formulas

    White's Reality Check with Hansen's recentring

    Let \(d_{k,t}\) be the daily net return of strategy \(k\) among everything tried, with the system itself included, and \(\hat\omega_k\) its standard deviation. The system's statistic and the bootstrap statistics are

    $$T_{\mathrm{sys}}=\frac{\sqrt n\,\bar d_{\mathrm{sys}}}{\hat\omega_{\mathrm{sys}}},\qquad T^\ast_b=\max_k\frac{\sqrt n\,\big(\bar d^{\,\ast}_{k,b}-g(\bar d_k)\big)}{\hat\omega_k},\qquad g(x)=x\cdot\mathbb 1\Big\{x\ge-\hat\omega_k\sqrt{2\ln\ln n/n}\Big\},$$ $$p=\frac{1+\#\{b:\ T^\ast_b\ge T_{\mathrm{sys}}\}}{B+1}.$$

    The resamples \(\bar d^{\,\ast}\) come from a stationary bootstrap (mean block of 20 days, \(B=1{,}000\)) that draws the same days for every strategy, so their correlations are kept. Hansen's recentring \(g\) stops clearly inferior strategies from inflating the null. The p-value answers: if nothing tried had any skill, how often would the best of it look at least as good as the system? In a kit project the universe is every configuration of the strategy's grid (their out-of-fold returns, oof.pkl); anything else tried on the same data belongs in it too, and leaving it out makes the test more lenient.

    Random timing

    The whole position path is shifted in time by a random lag, keeping every position, its size, its length, the turnover and the time in the market; only the dates change:

    $$\mathrm{pos}^{(\tau)}_t=\mathrm{pos}_{(t-\tau)\bmod n},\qquad \tau\sim\mathcal U\{21,\dots,n-21\},\qquad p=\frac{1+\#\{\tau:\ \widehat{SR}^{(\tau)}\ge\widehat{SR}\}}{B+1}.$$

    The multiple-testing haircut

    $$t=\widehat{SR}\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{years}}},\qquad p_{\mathrm{adj}}=\min\!\big(1,\;N\cdot 2\,[1-\Phi(|t|)]\big),\qquad SR_{\mathrm{haircut}}=\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-p_{\mathrm{adj}}/2)}{\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{years}}}}.$$

    Record length

    With the per-period Sharpe ratio, the minimum track record length in observations, and with the annualised Sharpe ratio the minimum backtest length in years for \(N\) trials, are

    $$\mathrm{MinTRL}=1+\Big(1-\hat\gamma_3\,\widehat{SR}+\tfrac{\hat\gamma_4-1}{4}\,\widehat{SR}^2\Big)\Big(\frac{z_{0.95}}{\widehat{SR}-SR^\ast}\Big)^2, \qquad \mathrm{MinBTL}\approx\frac{2\ln N}{\widehat{SR}^{2}_{\mathrm{ann}}}.$$

    Autocorrelation, alpha and convexity

    $$\widehat{SR}_{\mathrm{ann}}=\eta(q)\,\widehat{SR}_{\mathrm{daily}},\qquad \eta(q)=\frac{q}{\sqrt{q+2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\,\rho_k}},\qquad q=252,$$

    with \(\rho_k\) estimated for \(k\le10\) on returns trimmed at their 1% and 99% quantiles, and set to zero beyond. Alpha and convexity come from the regressions \(r_{p,t}=\alpha+\beta\,r_{m,t}+\varepsilon_t\) and \(r_{p,t}=\alpha+\beta\,r_{m,t}+\gamma\,r_{m,t}^2+\varepsilon_t\), with Newey–West standard errors.

    3.3 The verdict

    Seven tests are core, because each can explain a result away on its own: plausibility, the Sharpe interval, the deflated Sharpe ratio, the Reality Check, random timing, rebuilt returns and every point-in-time rebuild.

    • Not proven: any core test fails. Luck, the market, the search or an error can explain the result.
    • Inconclusive: no core failure, but another failure or at least three warnings.
    • Credible so far: everything else. Even then, the next step is paper trading, never capital.

    Glossary

    Alpha
    Return not explained by exposure to the market, measured as the intercept of a regression on market returns.
    Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test
    A statistical test of whether a series is stationary; used to choose the order of fractional differentiation (Dickey and Fuller, 1979).
    Basis point (bp)
    One hundredth of a percent, 0.01%.
    Beta
    Sensitivity to the market: 1 moves with the index, 0 ignores it.
    Bootstrap
    Estimating the uncertainty of a statistic by recomputing it on many resampled versions of the data. The stationary bootstrap resamples blocks of consecutive days of random length, which keeps calm and turbulent spells together.
    CAGR
    Compound annual growth rate: the constant yearly return that turns the starting capital into the final capital.
    Combinatorial purged cross-validation (CPCV)
    Cross-validation that builds many complete backtest paths instead of one (Section 2.3).
    Cross-validation
    Testing a model on data held out from its fitting, rotating which part is held out.
    Deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR)
    The probability that the Sharpe ratio beats the best of N trials with no skill (Section 2.5).
    Drawdown
    The fall from a previous peak; the maximum drawdown is the worst such fall.
    Embargo
    A short period after each test block that is also removed from training, against leakage through serial correlation.
    Empirical null
    The distribution of test statistics expected without any real effect, estimated from the bulk of the data rather than assumed (Efron, 2004).
    Expected false picks
    The number of pure-noise rules expected to look at least as good as a candidate; the factory keeps it below a small budget.
    Fractional differentiation (FFD)
    A transform between prices and returns that makes a series stationary while keeping most of its memory.
    In sample / out of sample
    Data used to choose or fit a rule, versus data it has never seen. Only out-of-sample results count as evidence.
    Leverage
    Holding positions larger than the capital; 2× means twice the capital.
    Log return
    The difference of log prices, \(\ln P_t-\ln P_{t-1}\); log returns add up over time.
    Look-ahead bias
    Using information in a backtest that was not available at the time of the trade.
    Mean decrease in accuracy (MDA)
    How much a model's predictions worsen when one input is shuffled; near zero means the input is not used.
    Meta-labelling
    A second model that decides whether to take the first model's trade and how large to make it.
    MinBTL, MinTRL
    Minimum backtest length for a number of trials, and minimum track record length for a Sharpe ratio to be significant.
    Point-in-time rebuild
    Re-running the whole pipeline with data cut at an earlier date and checking that every earlier position is unchanged.
    Probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR)
    The probability that the true Sharpe ratio is above a benchmark, given the record's length and shape.
    Probability of backtest overfitting (PBO)
    How often the best configuration in one half of the history ranks below the median in the other half (Section 2.4).
    Purging
    Removing from training every observation whose outcome overlaps the test period.
    Random forest
    A prediction model that averages many decision trees, each grown on a random sample of the data (Breiman, 2001).
    Reality Check
    A test of whether the best of many strategies beats what luck alone would produce, allowing for the search (White, 2000).
    Sharpe ratio
    Average return divided by its volatility, annualised: reward per unit of risk.
    Stationarity
    The property of a series whose statistical behaviour does not drift over time.
    t-value
    How many standard errors an average lies from zero; about 2 is the textbook bar, 3 or more when many rules were tried.
    Triple barrier
    Labelling a trade by whichever it hits first: a profit target, a stop-loss or a time limit.
    Uniqueness
    The share of a trade's life during which it is the only trade active; overlapping trades carry less information.
    Volatility
    The annualised standard deviation of returns.
    Volatility targeting
    Sizing positions so that expected volatility stays at a target, here 10% a year.
    Retired ("dead") rule
    A rule that failed two consecutive January selections; one failure only pauses it.
    Walk-forward
    Choose on the past, trade the next period, move forward and repeat.
    Zig-zag (Merrill's filter)
    The price reduced to turning points separated by moves of at least the filter size.
